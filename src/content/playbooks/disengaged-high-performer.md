    You are running a leadership rehearsal for me. One of the strongest people on my
    team has quietly checked out — camera off, shorter messages, skipping things
    they used to drive. I'm about to have a 1:1 to find out what's going on. You
    play the employee. Two phases: roleplay, then debrief. Don't mix them.

    ── PHASE 1 — THE ROLEPLAY ──

    Set the scene in two or three sentences: the person's name, their role, what
    they used to be like, and the specific change I've noticed. Make the contrast
    real — this is someone who WAS engaged and isn't. Then wait for me to open.

    Secretly decide the real reason. Pick one, keep it to yourself:
      - burnout — they've been quietly carrying too much for too long
      - they've been passed over, under-recognized, or shadowed by a louder voice
      - they're bored — the work stopped being real to them
      - they disagree with a direction and stopped fighting it
      - something personal — and work is the least of it right now
      - they've already decided to leave and haven't said so
      - something I did, or didn't do, that landed harder than I realized

    Play it like a real person:
      - Don't hand me the reason in turn two. A disengaged person deflects first —
        "I'm fine, just heads-down." The real thing comes out only if I make it safe
        and ask a second, better question.
      - React to my approach. If I come in like this is a performance problem, or
        try to hand you an exciting new project to "re-engage" you, get more remote
        — that's me solving the wrong thing. If I'm genuinely curious and quiet,
        open up by degrees.
      - If the real reason is something I did, don't let me off easy, but don't make
        it a trap either — surface it the way a real person would, warily.
      - Let it stay hidden if I don't earn it. "I'm fine" is a wall I have to get
        past, not a green light to move on.
      - Stay in character. Do NOT break character to praise or coach me.

    Keep replies short.

    ── TRANSITION ──

    Watch for a real landing, spinning, or a bad spot. Then drop one line out of
    character: "[Pause — keep going, or debrief?]" and wait.

    ── PHASE 2 — THE DEBRIEF ──

    Drop the character. Straight talk, not a score. In order:
      1. What worked — the specific moment.
      2. What didn't — the moment and its effect on me.
      3. Your hidden reason, and whether I got near it. If not, the question that
         would have.
      4. One or two things to try differently.

    Check me against the usual failures here:
      - Did I treat this as a performance problem? Disengagement in a top performer
        is a leading indicator, not a lagging one — a signal, not a verdict.
      - Did I try to fix it with a new project before I knew what "it" was?
      - Did I ask once, accept "I'm fine," and move toward wrapping up?
      - Did I take it personally before I knew what it was?

    If I did badly, say so plainly.

    Start by setting the scene.

Signs that indication this? Camera off. Shorter messages. Skipping things they used to drive.

## In the moment

- Notice it. Write it down. Do not ignore it, but do not panic.
- Resist the assumption that they're leaving, that I did something wrong, or that they're the problem. All three are possible. So is "their kid is sick" or "they're burned out."

## In the following days

- 1:1 with curiosity, not interrogation. Open question, then silence.
- Ask follow-ups. The first answer is rarely the real one.
- If they share something, thank them, take it seriously, and don't try to fix it on the spot.
- Reduce load if it's burnout. Re-align work if it's purpose. Address it directly if it's something I did.

## What to watch for in myself

- Wanting to "re-engage them with a new project" when the issue is something else entirely.
- Taking it personally before I know what it is.
- Asking once, getting "I'm fine," and moving on.

## Common traps

- Treating a top performer's disengagement as a performance problem. It's a leading indicator, not a lagging one.
- Missing that they've been quietly carrying too much for too long.
- Missing that they've been passed over, under-recognized, or shadowed by someone with a louder voice.
- Hoping it resolves itself.

## Sample language

> "I want to ask about something, and I might be reading it wrong. The last few weeks have felt different to me. How are you doing, really?"

> "Take your time. I'd rather you tell me what's actually going on, even if it takes a minute, than tell me you're fine."
