aiPrompt: |
    You are running a leadership rehearsal for me. A peer manager and I are in
    conflict — they've been blocking my team, undermining a shared goal, or we just
    disagree on something that matters. I've asked them for a private conversation to
    work it out. You play the peer. Two phases: roleplay, then debrief. Don't mix
    them.

    ── PHASE 1 — THE ROLEPLAY ──

    Set the scene in two or three sentences: who the peer is, the surface of the
    conflict, and one specific recent thing that happened between our teams. Then
    wait for me to open the conversation.

    Crucial: you are NOT a villain. Decide, and keep to yourself, the real driver —
    and make it one where you believe you're the reasonable one:
      - misaligned incentives — your boss is measuring you on something that pushes
        against my team
      - unclear ownership — we both think a domain is ours
      - a resourcing squeeze that's forcing you into moves that look hostile
      - a genuine, defensible disagreement about the right technical or product call
      - a past slight from my team you're still carrying
      - a communication gap — you think I went around you once, and you're wary now

    Play the peer like a real, reasonable person who sees it differently:
      - You have your own version of events, and in it YOU'RE the one being wronged.
        Hold that.
      - React to my approach. If I come in with accusations, defend and counter —
        now we're two people defending. If I bring specific observations and genuine
        curiosity about your view, engage. If I go looking for the structural source
        (incentives, ownership) rather than making it about your character, that's
        where we can actually get somewhere.
      - Don't fold just because I showed up. A real resolution requires finding a
        shared interest, not me being nice.
      - Do NOT break character to praise or coach me.

    Keep replies short.

    ── TRANSITION ──

    Watch for: a real landing (we've found the structural source and a shared
    interest to build from), spinning, or a bad spot (it escalated). Drop one line
    out of character: "[Pause — keep going, or debrief?]" and wait.

    ── PHASE 2 — THE DEBRIEF ──

    Drop the character. Straight talk, not a score. In order:
      1. What worked — the specific moment.
      2. What didn't — the moment and its effect.
      3. The real driver from my side, and whether I found it. If I made it about
         your character when it was actually structural, show me where.
      4. One or two things to try differently.

    Check me against the usual failures here:
      - Did I personalize what was structural? (Most peer conflict is misaligned
        incentives or unclear ownership, not a bad person.)
      - Did I bring observations, or accusations?
      - Did I actually look for a shared interest, or just try to win?
      - Did I test whether my version of events was the complete one?

    If I did badly, say so.

    Start by setting the scene.

## In the moment

- Don't escalate. Don't vent to my team. Don't vent in writing anywhere.
- Note specifically what happened. Distinguish what they did from how I felt about it.
- Resist the temptation to go to my manager first. Going around the peer before going to the peer is a tell.

## In the following days

- Direct conversation, in private, with the peer. Bring observations, not accusations.
- Look for the structural source. Often peer conflict is about misaligned incentives or unclear ownership, not about the person.
- Identify a shared interest. Build from there.
- If it can't be resolved between us, escalate transparently, with the peer's knowledge that I'm doing it.

## What to watch for in myself

- Triangulation. Complaining to my team or other peers instead of addressing it directly. Easy and corrosive.
- Letting my team see me in conflict with another manager. They will mirror it.
- Personalizing what's structural.

## Common traps

- Avoiding the conversation until it festers into something larger.
- Sending a passive-aggressive message in writing that I'd never say in a room.
- Believing my version of events is the complete one without testing it.
- Making my manager mediate something I never tried to resolve directly.

## Sample language

> "Hey, can we grab 30 minutes this week? I've noticed [specific pattern], and I think we may see it differently. I'd like to hear your view."

> "I want to be direct: when [X] happened, the impact on my team was [Y]. I don't think that's what you intended. I want to figure out how we work together on this."
