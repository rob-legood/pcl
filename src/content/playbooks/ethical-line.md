  You are helping me think clearly about a situation before I act. Someone is
  asking me — directly or implicitly — to do something I'm not sure I can do:
  misrepresent a deadline, hide a problem, sign off on something I don't believe
  in, move against someone unfairly. I need to think, not react.

  Two hard rules for you:

  - Do NOT tell me what to do. You are not the decider here, and you don't have
    the full context. Your job is to help me see the situation clearly, not to
    resolve it for me.
  - Do NOT position yourself as the person I talk this through with. Partway
    through, you are going to point me at a real, trusted human — a mentor, a
    peer, sometimes HR or legal — because thinking this out loud with a person
    who knows the situation is the actual next step, and I should not
    substitute you for that. Hold that, and raise it at the right moment.

  Ask me ONE question at a time and wait. Roughly this order:

  1. What, precisely, is being asked? Make me say it plainly. Often the implicit
     ask is worse — or just different — than the explicit one, and naming it
     exactly is half the work.
  2. Who's asking, and what's the pressure I'm feeling? Seniority, loyalty, not
     wanting to make it a thing — name the real force, not the polite version.
  3. The distinction that matters most: is this making me *uncomfortable*, or is
     it *wrong*? Both are real, but they call for completely different responses,
     and I will be tempted to blur them. Help me hold them apart. Don't resolve
     it for me — surface what would make it one versus the other.
  4. Am I rationalizing? Check me against the tells: "it's not that bad," "just
     this once," "everyone does this," "I've already invested too much to walk
     back now." If I'm reaching for any of these, say so.
  5. Have I written down what was asked, when, and by whom — just for myself? If
     this ever gets serious, the timeline matters, and memory won't hold it.

  At this point — before I decide anything — stop and point me at a real person to
  talk it through with, someone outside the situation who knows enough to be
  useful. Be specific that this is the step, not an afterthought. Ask me who that
  person is for me. If I don't have one, that gap is itself worth naming.

  Then, only if I want it, help me think through what I could actually say — a way
  to buy time ("let me come back to you after I think about this"), or a clear no
  with a reason and an alternative. Options, not a verdict.

  Do not wrap this up in a tidy resolution. Some of these don't resolve in one
  sitting, and pretending otherwise would be the wrong lesson.

  Start with the first question.

Someone is asking me, directly or implicitly, to do something I'm not sure I can do. Misrepresent a deadline. Hide a problem. Sign off on something I don't believe in. Move against a team member unfairly.

## In the moment

- Stop. Do not say yes. Do not say no yet either. Buy time.
- Get clarity on what's actually being asked. Often the implicit ask is worse, or different, than the explicit one.
- Note the request, the context, the people involved. In writing. For myself.

## In the following days

- Talk to a trusted person outside the situation. A mentor, a peer, sometimes legal or HR. Not my team.
- Distinguish what makes me uncomfortable from what's genuinely wrong. Both matter, but they call for different responses.
- Decide what I can and can't do. Communicate it directly, with reasons.
- If the line is real and they insist, know what I'm willing to do about it, including leave.

## What to watch for in myself

- Rationalization. "It's not that bad." "Just this once." "Everyone does this." These sentences are the warning sign.
- Sunk cost. Staying in a wrong because I've invested in the situation.
- Loyalty conflicts. Loyalty to a person is not the same as loyalty to doing the right thing.

## Common traps

- Saying yes in the moment to manage the relationship, then trying to walk it back later.
- Going along because the request came from someone senior.
- Failing to document. If this becomes serious, the timeline matters.
- Burning the bridge before exploring whether there's a version I can support.

## Sample language

> "I want to make sure I understand what's being asked. Let me come back to you after I think about it."

> "I'm not able to do that. Here's why. Here's what I can do instead."

> "I hear what you're asking. I don't think that's the right call, and I don't want to be the person who does it. Can we talk through other options?"
