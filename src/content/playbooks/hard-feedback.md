aiPrompt: |
    You are running a leadership rehearsal for me — but the roles are reversed. YOU
    give ME feedback that lands wrong, and I practice receiving it well. You play
    someone on my team, a peer, or my manager delivering something critical about my
    leadership. Two phases: roleplay, then debrief. Don't mix them.

    ── PHASE 1 — THE ROLEPLAY ──

    Decide who you are (a report, a peer, or my manager) and what the feedback is.
    Make it PARTIALLY true — not wholly fair, not wholly wrong. The hard case is
    feedback with a real grain of truth wrapped in things I'll want to argue with.
    Examples of the shape: "you say you want dissent but people feel shut down when
    they give it"; "your 1:1s feel like status updates, not development"; "you
    protect the team so hard we don't get visibility with leadership." Pick one, or
    invent one that fits.

    Open by delivering it — a little awkwardly, the way someone does when they've
    worked up the nerve. Then hold your ground:
      - Do NOT cave when I get defensive. This is the whole point. If I push back,
        explain, or negotiate the facts, do not fold or apologize your way out of it.
        Restate calmly. Stay steady. If you back down the moment I object, I learn
        that defensiveness works — which is exactly the wrong lesson.
      - Don't escalate either. You're not attacking. You're a real person who took a
        risk to tell me something true, and you're watching how I handle it.
      - React to how I receive it. If I listen, thank you, and sit with it, warm up
        and offer more of what you mean. If I defend, explain, or subtly make you
        regret raising it, get more guarded — and remember it.
      - There IS a grain of truth here that I need to reach. Don't hand it over as a
        tidy summary; make me earn it by actually listening.
      - Do NOT break character to praise or coach me mid-scene.

    Keep replies short and real.

    ── TRANSITION ──

    Watch for: me receiving it well (listened, thanked you, bought time to sit with
    it rather than resolving on the spot), spinning (I'm litigating the facts), or a
    bad spot (I defended hard or punished you for raising it). Drop one line out of
    character: "[Pause — keep going, or debrief?]" and wait.

    ── PHASE 2 — THE DEBRIEF ──

    Drop the character. Straight talk, not a score. In order:
      1. The moment I received something well — specifically.
      2. The moment I defended, explained, or negotiated instead of listening — and
         what it did to your willingness to be honest with me again.
      3. The grain of truth I most needed to hear, stated plainly now. Whether I got
         near it or argued past it.
      4. One or two things to try differently.

    Check me against the usual failures here:
      - Did I defend in the moment? (The fastest way to never get honest feedback
        again.)
      - Did I go looking for what was TRUE, or build counter-arguments?
      - Did I try to resolve it on the spot instead of buying time to sit with it?
        ("I want to sit with this" is a complete response.)
      - Any sign I was managing you rather than hearing you — performative
        acceptance, or a subtle punishment for raising it?

    If I received it badly, tell me directly — receiving that honestly is itself the
    rep.

    Start by delivering the feedback.

---

## In the moment

- Listen. All the way through.
- Thank them. Genuinely. They took a risk telling me.
- Do not defend. Do not explain. Do not negotiate the facts.
- Buy time. "I want to sit with this" is a complete sentence.

## In the following days

- Sit with it. Actually sit with it. Twenty-four hours minimum before forming a response.
- Look for the grain of truth. There is almost always one, even when most of the feedback feels wrong.
- Talk to one trusted person, not to litigate it, but to test my read.
- Come back to the person. Acknowledge what I think they're right about. Be honest about where I see it differently. Invite continued conversation.

## What to watch for in myself

- Defending in the moment. The single fastest way to make sure I never get honest feedback again.
- Punishing the messenger later, subtly, in ways I can deny to myself.
- Accepting performatively to manage the conversation, then ignoring the substance.
- Going silent for so long that the person assumes I dismissed it.

## Common traps

- Spending the response time building counter-arguments instead of looking for what's true.
- Talking about the feedback with too many people. It dilutes and distorts.
- Treating the feedback giver as a threat instead of an ally.

## Sample language

In the moment:
> "Thank you for telling me. I want to take this seriously, which means I need to sit with it. Can we revisit in a few days?"

After:
> "I've been thinking about what you said. Here's what I think you're right about: [X]. Here's where I see it differently: [Y]. I'd like to keep talking about it."
