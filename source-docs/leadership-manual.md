# Rob's Leadership Manual: First 90 Days and Beyond

A personal playbook for stepping into a new director or software engineering manager role.

---

## How to Use This Manual

This is a living document. Treat each section as a checklist of milestones and reminders, not a script. As you learn what works for you, edit ruthlessly — delete what doesn't apply, add what does, and capture lessons in the **Field Notes** section at the bottom so the next transition is easier than this one.

Suggested rhythm:
- Review the relevant phase at the **start** of each week.
- Update Field Notes at the **end** of each week (5 minutes, max).
- Revisit the whole manual after each transition and prune.

---

## Phase 0 — Before Day 1 (Pre-Start)

**Goal:** Show up grounded, not anxious. You can't control much yet, so focus on what you can.

- [ ] Research the company publicly: latest earnings or funding, blog posts, press, Glassdoor signal, recent product launches.
- [ ] Read anything you were given: org chart, strategy decks, handbook, recent all-hands recordings.
- [ ] Identify (from LinkedIn + your recruiter) the names and rough backgrounds of: your manager, your direct reports, your peer managers, your skip-level.
- [ ] Draft a one-page **self-introduction**: who you are, what you've done, what you care about, what to expect from you in the first few weeks. Keep it human, not a resume recap. (See template below.)
- [ ] Block recurring time on your calendar *before* it gets eaten: deep work, 1:1s, reflection time, exercise.
- [ ] Decide on a personal **knowledge capture system** before info starts flowing — Notion, Obsidian, a single doc, whatever. Don't trust memory.
- [ ] Rest. Seriously. The first month will spend energy faster than you expect.

## Phase 1 — Week 1: Land

**Goal:** Meet people, set up logistics, project calm curiosity. Resist the urge to have opinions.

### People
- [ ] Meeting with your manager — clarify: what does success look like at 30/60/90 days? What's the one thing they wish their last person had done? How do they like to communicate?
- [ ] First 1:1s with each direct report (30-45 min). Goal is listening, not directing. (See 1:1 template below.)
- [ ] Identify peer managers and request intro chats this week or next.
- [ ] Identify your skip-level and ask your manager when an intro makes sense.
- [ ] Deliver self-intro to the team (in person, on a call, or async via a written post — match the team's medium).

### Logistics
- [ ] Access: all repos, CI/CD, monitoring, on-call rotation, docs sites, dashboards, communication channels.
- [ ] Subscribe to the channels and mailing lists your team is in.
- [ ] Get added to relevant recurring meetings; decline anything that isn't clearly useful yet (you can opt back in).

### Posture
- Default to questions, not answers.
- Write down things you'd change. Do not act on them yet.
- Notice what energizes and drains each team member in conversation — useful data later.

## Phase 2 — Days 8-30: Listen

**Goal:** Build a high-resolution map of the people, the work, the systems, and the politics. Earn trust by being curious and useful in small ways.

### Establish 1:1 cadence
- [ ] Weekly 1:1s with each direct report, 30 min, recurring. Their agenda first, then yours.
- [ ] Bi-weekly or monthly with key peers and skip-level.
- [ ] A shared 1:1 doc per report — running notes, action items, topics for next time.

### Listening tour
Run a structured set of conversations across the team and adjacent teams. Suggested questions:
- What's working well that we should protect?
- What's broken or frustrating?
- If you were in my seat, what would you do first?
- Who else should I talk to?
- What do you wish leadership knew?
- What's the biggest risk to this team or product in the next 6 months?

Track responses in a single doc; themes will emerge by the end of week 3.

### Get dirty with the code and systems
- [ ] Clone the main repos. Build them locally. Run the tests. Note where setup is painful — that's a signal.
- [ ] Read the README and architecture docs; flag what's stale.
- [ ] Pair with an engineer on a real task — not to ship, but to feel the developer experience.
- [ ] Shadow on-call for one rotation if feasible. Read recent incident reviews.
- [ ] Inventory the major services / repos / domains. Sketch a one-page architecture diagram for yourself.

### Understand the business
- [ ] Re-read the company mission, vision, values. Find out how recently they were updated and whether anyone references them.
- [ ] Understand how your team's work ladders up to company priorities. If you can't articulate it in one sentence, you don't understand it yet.
- [ ] Learn the revenue or impact model. What does a customer pay for? What metric does the company live or die by?
- [ ] Identify the company's current strategic bets and how the team fits in.

### Audit existing metrics
- [ ] Inventory what's already measured: business KPIs, product metrics, engineering health metrics (DORA, incident rates, on-call load), people metrics (eNPS, attrition).
- [ ] For each metric: who owns it, where it lives, who looks at it, what action it drives.
- [ ] Note: which decisions are made with data vs. without? Which metrics are vanity vs. actionable?

### Stakeholder map
- [ ] List everyone whose work depends on yours or whose work you depend on. Categorize by influence and interest. Plan how often you'll engage each.

### Personal systems
- [ ] Stand up a todo / work tracker (e.g., a daily list in a doc, Things, Linear, Todoist — pick one and commit for 90 days).
- [ ] Weekly review ritual: 30 minutes Friday afternoon — what happened, what's next, what's blocked.
- [ ] Reading queue for things you want to come back to.
- [ ] Decision log: when you make a non-trivial decision, write down the context, options, choice, and expected outcome. Revisit in 3 months.

## Phase 3 — Days 31-60: Diagnose

**Goal:** Synthesize what you've learned. Start forming a point of view. Begin small, visible improvements.

- [ ] Write a private **assessment doc** for yourself: state of the team, top 5 risks, top 5 opportunities, what kind of leadership the moment calls for (turnaround? steady state? acceleration?).
- [ ] Share a sanitized version with your manager and ask for pushback.
- [ ] Identify 1-3 **quick wins** — visible improvements you can deliver in 2-4 weeks that don't require strategy buy-in. Examples: fix a hated meeting, clarify on-call expectations, kill a useless metric, improve a broken doc.
- [ ] Begin defining your own metrics — what *you* want to track that isn't already tracked. Examples: % of 1:1s where the report drove the agenda, time-to-first-PR for new hires, weekly hours in meetings vs. deep work.
- [ ] Identify any urgent personnel issues. Don't sit on them, but don't fire from the hip either.
- [ ] Confirm or revise your understanding of the team's mandate with your manager.

---

## Phase 4 — Days 61-90: Shape

**Goal:** Move from observer to author. Commit to a direction. Communicate it clearly.

- [ ] Draft a **team operating plan**: mission, priorities for the next 1-2 quarters, what you'll stop doing, what success looks like.
- [ ] Socialize the plan with peers and manager *before* announcing it to the team. Refine.
- [ ] Present the plan to the team. Invite challenge. Adjust.
- [ ] Define and communicate your **operating rhythms**: meeting cadence, decision-making process, how you'll give and receive feedback, what you'll escalate and what you won't.
- [ ] Set up a recurring forum for team announcements and visibility (weekly written update, monthly all-hands, whatever fits).
- [ ] Begin the first goal-setting cycle under your leadership if the calendar allows.

## Ongoing Operating System

Once the first 90 days are behind you, what does steady-state look like?

### Weekly
- 1:1s with all direct reports.
- Friday review: what shipped, what's stuck, what's next week's top 3.
- One written update to your team or broader org (even short).

### Monthly
- Skip-level conversations (rotate).
- Metric review with the team.
- One "no agenda" coffee with a peer in another part of the org.
- Personal reflection: am I doing the job I was hired to do, or am I hiding in the comfortable parts?

### Quarterly
- Goal review and reset.
- 360-style feedback solicitation (informal is fine).
- Career conversations with each direct report.
- Update this manual.

### Annually
- Performance reviews.
- Compensation cycle.
- Strategy planning for the team.
- A real vacation. Disconnect.

## Templates

### Self-Introduction (one-pager)
1. **Who I am** — short bio, what brought me here, one or two genuinely human details.
2. **What I've done** — 2-3 lines of relevant prior experience, framed by what you learned, not titles.
3. **What I believe about leadership / engineering / teams** — three to five principles. Be specific.
4. **What to expect from me in the first 90 days** — mostly listening, some questions, no big changes.
5. **What I'll need from you** — honesty, patience, context, candor when I'm wrong.
6. **How I work** — communication preferences, availability, response times.

### 1:1 Template
- How are you, actually?
- What's on your mind? (Their agenda first.)
- What's blocking you?
- What feedback do you have for me?
- Anything from me: [my agenda items]
- Action items + owners.

## Things to Watch Out For

- Making big changes before you've earned the right to.
- Confusing motion for progress.
- Becoming the bottleneck. Your job is to multiply, not to do.
- Letting your calendar fill with meetings that aren't moving the team forward.
- Withholding hard feedback because it's uncomfortable.
- Forgetting that your direct reports read your mood, your calendar, and your Slack response times as signals.
